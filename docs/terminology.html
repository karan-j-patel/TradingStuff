Trading Platform Terminology

Terms used in this codebase, defined for an engineer who has not traded. Each entry says what the word means and why it matters here, not in general.

The order book

Everything in the fast strand is about this one structure. A worked example, real prices from the GOOG data in data/fixtures/:

level 1579.35100579.12100
level 2579.4050579.0041
level 3579.55300578.991

Highlighted row is the inside. Spread here is 579.28 − 579.19 = 9 cents.

Order book
Every resting offer to buy or sell, sorted by price. Not a database of trades. It is the set of intentions that have not traded yet.
Bid / Ask
Bid is what someone will pay. Ask (or offer) is what someone will sell for. Buyers rest on the bid side, sellers on the ask side.
Level
One occupied price. Level 0 is the best price on that side, level 1 the next price out. Levels are occupied prices, not ticks, so level 2 to level 3 may be one cent or fifty. Here: our LOBSTER files are "level 10" files, ten levels a side. That limit is why level 9 is the only one that ever disagrees.
Inside / Top of book
Level 0 on both sides. The best bid and the best ask. Here: the number that decides whether an execution simulation is honest. Most orders never touch anything deeper.
Spread
Ask minus bid. The price of demanding immediacy: buy at the ask and sell at the bid instantly, and you have lost the spread. Here: the central cost of the fast strand. Execution wins by avoiding it. Prediction must overcome it.
Depth
How much size rests at each level. A deep book absorbs large orders without moving; a thin one does not.
Limit order / Market order
A limit order names a price and waits. A market order takes whatever is available now. Limit orders build the book; market orders consume it.
Passive / Aggressive
Passive means resting in the book and waiting to be hit, which earns the spread. Aggressive means crossing it to trade now, which pays the spread. Here: the whole execution thesis. Passive fills turn a cost into a gain, but only if they actually fill.
Queue position
Orders at the same price fill in arrival order. Two traders posting the same price get very different outcomes depending on who arrived first. Here: the thing an order-level feed gives you and a price-level feed never can. OrderBook::queue_ahead returns an error rather than a guess on price-level data, because a strategy that thinks it knows its queue position when it does not will overestimate its own fill rate.
Fill / Fill rate
A fill is an order actually trading. Fill rate is the fraction of passive orders that fill rather than being cancelled unfilled. Here: the single unmeasured number the fast strand depends on. Gate F0 forbids any profit claim until it is measured in paper trading. It cannot be measured from historical data, because that data does not contain your orders.
Hidden (undisplayed) order
Liquidity resting in the book that the feed does not show, usually priced inside the spread for price improvement. Here: LOBSTER message type 5. Applying these to the visible book is the classic reconstruction bug. We proved empirically that they never move the visible book: rows where LOBSTER's book does not move number 3,913 for GOOG, and GOOG has exactly 3,913 hidden executions.
Crossed book
Best bid at or above best ask, which is nonsense within one venue because those orders would have traded. Here: a bug detector. It caught the DeepReader ordering bug, and it caught the fact that continuous LOBSTER replay is impossible.
MBO / MBP
Market-by-order reports individual orders and their identities. Market-by-price reports only the total at each price. MBO costs money; MBP is often free. Here: LOBSTER is MBO, IEX DEEP is MBP. Only MBO can answer queue position, which is why the crate tracks which one it is holding.
NBBO
National Best Bid and Offer: the best price across all US venues, not one venue's own book. Here: spread capture must be measured against this. IEX alone is 2-3% of volume and quotes wider, so measuring against IEX would flatter every result. Our capture showed AAPL at 9 cents on IEX; that is not what a routed order pays.
Halt
Trading stopped, usually for volatility or pending news. Orders keep resting but do not trade, and prices commonly gap on resume.

Execution quality

Arrival price
The price at the moment you decided to trade. The honest benchmark, because it is the last price not yet contaminated by your own trading.
Implementation shortfall
Difference between the price you decided at and the price you actually got, including fees and unfilled remainder. Here: the measure of the execution layer. Benchmarked against a naive market order placed at decision time.
Slippage
Informally, the same idea: the gap between expected and achieved price.
Markout
Where the price went after your fill, measured at fixed horizons such as 1s, 10s and 60s. Here: Gate F1 requires all three. Capturing two cents of spread and then watching the price move three cents against you is a loss dressed as a saving.
Adverse selection
The risk that whoever traded with you knew something you did not. Your passive orders fill precisely when someone wants the other side badly. Here: the reason passive execution is not free money, and what markout measures.
Realized vs quoted spread
Quoted is the spread on the screen. Realized is what you actually captured after the price moved against you. Realized is always the smaller number.
Latency
Delay between deciding and the exchange acknowledging. Reported as a distribution, never a single number, because the tail is when it matters. Here: a backtest that acts the instant a signal appears is fiction. Measured decision-to-submit time is applied as a delay in replay.
Parent / child order
A parent is what you want to trade. Children are the slices actually sent, to avoid moving the price with one large order.

Statistics and research method

Cross-sectional vs time-series
Time-series asks "will AAPL go up," using its own history. Cross-sectional asks "of these thousands of stocks, which will beat the others." Here: this distinction defines the slow strand. Time-series prediction of a single asset is a well-documented dead end. Cross-sectional prediction works, modestly, which is the Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020) result.
Sharpe ratio
Return divided by volatility. Return per unit of risk. Higher is better; 1.0 is respectable, above 2 is rare and usually wrong.
Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR)
A probability between 0 and 1 that the edge is real rather than the luckiest of everything you tried. It is not a return figure. Here: non-negotiable rule 1. No raw Sharpe appears without it and without the trial count behind it.
Trial count / multiple testing
Test enough strategies and one looks brilliant by chance. The count of everything tried is what converts a Sharpe into an honest probability. Here: the append-only, hash-chained trials/ log. A 100-point hyperparameter grid is 100 trials, not one.
Lookahead bias
Using information that was not available at the time. Usually accidental, via reindexing, forward-filling, or a careless date join.
Point-in-time
Data as it was known on the date in question, not as it was later corrected.
As-reported vs restated
As-reported is the number the company first published. Restated is the corrected version. Backtesting on restated numbers uses knowledge nobody had. Here: Sharadar dimension ARQ, never MRQ.
Survivorship bias
Studying only companies that still exist. The failures dropped out of the dataset, so returns look better than reality.
Delisting return
What you actually got when a holding stopped trading. Usually missing from vendor data, and usually very negative when the cause was failure. Here: imputed with published conventions, −30% NYSE/AMEX (Shumway 1997), −55% Nasdaq (Shumway & Warther 1999), 0% for mergers with unknown terms.
Factor
A stock characteristic that has historically predicted relative returns, such as value, momentum, or quality.
Book-to-market
Accounting net worth divided by market price. High means the market values the company below its books. The classic value measure.
Accruals
The gap between reported profit and actual cash. Large accruals mean profit exists on paper more than in the bank, and predict weak future returns.
Decile sort
Rank all stocks on a characteristic, cut into ten equal buckets, compare the top bucket against the bottom. The standard way to show a factor works.
Turnover
How much of the portfolio is replaced each period. Every unit of turnover pays the spread, so turnover is the bridge between a signal and its cost.
Baseline
The dumb alternative your result must beat. Equal-weight buy-and-hold, random ranking at matched turnover, and a linear model on the same features. Here: rule 5. If gradient boosting does not beat ridge regression net of costs, that is a publishable finding, not a failure.

Short selling

Short
Borrow a share, sell it, buy it back later. Profits if the price falls. Losses are unbounded, since the price can rise without limit.
Borrow / Easy-to-borrow
You must locate shares to borrow before shorting. Easy-to-borrow names are freely available at no fee; hard-to-borrow names cost, sometimes heavily.
Short rebate
Interest earned on the cash from the short sale. Institutions earn near the policy rate. Retail earns zero. Here: a 4-5% per year drag that is larger than typical borrow fees and routinely unmodelled. Rule 3 requires applying it.
Buy-in
The lender recalls the shares and you are forced to close, at whatever price the market offers.

Data formats and plumbing

Tick data
Every individual event, quote change and trade, rather than daily summaries. Billions of rows a year.
ITCH / DEEP
Exchange feed protocols. Nasdaq TotalView-ITCH is order-level. IEX DEEP is price-level and free.
LOBSTER
An academic service that reconstructs order books from ITCH and publishes both the message stream and their own reconstructed book. Here: our correctness oracle. Feeding their messages to our book and diffing against their output is the one test that can catch a mistake we also made in our own test.
pcapng
A raw network packet capture format. Our IEX fixture is 13GB of it, gzipped, and is streamed rather than decompressed to disk.
Parquet
A columnar file format. Fast to read one column across millions of rows. Here: the only boundary between Rust and Python. Rust writes it, Python reads it, neither calls the other.
DuckDB
A SQL engine that queries Parquet files in place. Here: a query engine pointed at files, not a database to load into. There is no import step.
Two rules worth remembering, because they explain most decisions in this codebase.

Rule 1. No Sharpe ratio ever appears without its deflated counterpart and the trial count behind it. A Sharpe presented as performance with no trial count is a hard error, not a style violation.

Rule 6. No language model in the decision path. LLMs may write code, turn text into numeric features, and explain results already computed. They may not decide what to trade or forecast returns. A model reading "CEO resigns" has no idea what the market already priced, and will produce a confident directional call from information that does not contain the answer.
